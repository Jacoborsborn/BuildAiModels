How to start a web design agency with no experience

Published July 2026 9 min read

Five years ago, starting a web design agency meant one of two things: learn to code for a year, or subcontract the work and keep pennies on every job. Both barriers are gone, and hardly anyone has adjusted to what that means. An AI coding agent will now take a plain-English instruction and return a complete, mobile-first website in hours, which sounds like it should have destroyed the web design business. It did the opposite. It destroyed the price of the build and left the actual business of finding clients, winning trust and looking after sites month after month wide open to anyone willing to do it. Roughly £25 a month of tooling now covers what an employed developer used to do. The people winning with this aren't designers or coders. They're the ones who picked a patch, made the calls, and sold an ongoing service instead of a one-off build. Here is the whole route, honestly, including the parts that are still hard.

Quick answer

To start a web design agency with no experience in 2026, you don't learn to code, you run a process: pick a local niche, use an AI coding agent such as Claude Code to build demo sites in hours, and sell a done-for-you plan at £50–150 a month instead of one-off builds. The tooling costs about £25/month. The hard part isn't the websites; it's consistent outreach.

Sell retainers, not websites

A new web design agency in 2026 should sell a done-for-you monthly plan at £50–150 a month, with hosting, security and edits included, because AI has collapsed the price of the build itself while the value of ongoing care has gone up.

This is the single decision that separates an agency from a string of odd jobs, and it's worth getting right before you build anything.

Why the one-off build is a dead end

When a website took eighty hours to hand-build, charging £1,500–£3,000 once made sense. Now that the build takes hours, one-off pricing puts you in a race to the bottom against every DIY builder advert on television. Worse, a one-off client pays you once and disappears, so every month starts back at zero. The market has split: the cheap one-off end belongs to Wix and Squarespace now, and the end that's growing is done-for-you. These are businesses that don't want a login, a dashboard or a tutorial. They want it handled.

What the monthly plan includes

The plan that sells is simple: I build it, I host it, I keep it working, and small changes are included, from £50 a month for a one-page site and £100–150 for a full site. Hosting at this scale costs you pennies, so almost all of that is margin for the ongoing relationship. Ten clients at £100 a month is £12,000 a year of recurring revenue, and each new client stacks on top of the last instead of replacing it.

Why small businesses say yes

A £1,500 invoice is a board meeting for a sole trader; £50 a month is a line in the direct debits next to their van insurance. It's also directly comparable to what many of them already pay lead platforms, often £60–120 a month for shared leads they don't own. A flat monthly fee for an asset with their own name on it is an easy comparison to win.

The toolchain: what replaces the skills you don't have

The working stack for a no-experience web design agency is an AI coding agent (Claude Code), a free GitHub account and free static hosting such as Cloudflare Pages: roughly £25 a month all-in, most of which is the AI subscription.

There's no web design software to master and no course in HTML required. The stack is three accounts and an afternoon of setup.

What the AI genuinely does

Given a clear instruction (the trade, the town, the photos, the phone number), a coding agent writes the entire site: layout, copy, mobile behaviour, contact forms, the lot. It will rebuild a prospect's dated website onto a modern template in an evening. It handles the technical plumbing (domains, deployment, forms) with you directing in plain English. That's not a gimmick; it's the same class of tool professional developers now use daily.

What it doesn't do: the honest bit

The AI will not find you prospects you haven't pointed it at, pick up the phone, or follow up politely two weeks after a "maybe". It also won't stop you shipping something generic: you still make the calls on taste, meaning which photos, which testimonials, what the first screen says. Anyone promising the whole business runs itself is selling you something. The build is automated; the business isn't.

What it costs to run

Call it £25–50 a month to run the whole operation, less than most people's petrol for a week. The startup cost is genuinely the lowest of any service business I can think of.

Pick a niche and a patch before you build anything

The easiest first market is local trade businesses (plumbers, electricians, decorators) inside a defined territory, qualified by two signals: a visibly dated website and a mobile number on it.

"Everyone with a business" is not a niche, and a beginner competing everywhere competes nowhere. Constraint is what makes the outreach workable.

Why local trades

Trades are the ideal first niche for a reason: demand for their work is local and constant, an enormous number of them have websites built in 2012 or none at all, and they're used to paying monthly, often resentfully, for marketing on lead platforms. You're not educating them about why being findable matters; you're offering a better deal on something they already buy.

The two-signal qualification bar

A dated website means there's a visible, undeniable problem you can show them. A mobile number on that site means you can actually reach the decision-maker: no gatekeeper, no info@ inbox where messages go to die. Every prospect must clear both bars before they earn a place on your calling sheet. This one rule filters out most of the wasted effort beginners burn on prospects who were never reachable.

How big a territory

A county, or a city and its surrounding towns, is plenty: enough to hold hundreds of qualified prospects across five or six trades, small enough that "I'm based near you" is true on every call. Local familiarity closes deals: naming their town, knowing their patch, being someone they could theoretically meet.

Get your first client with a demo, not a portfolio

You don't need a portfolio to win a first client. You need one demo: a real prospect's existing website rebuilt onto a modern template and sent to them as a live link before you've ever spoken.

The chicken-and-egg problem (no clients without a portfolio, no portfolio without clients) dissolves when the build costs you an evening instead of a month.

The swap-and-send demo

Take a qualified prospect from your sheet and rebuild their actual business (their trade, their town, their photos) as a real, scrollable site on your template. Then send them the link. A screenshot is forgettable and a pitch is ignorable, but their own business looking better than it's ever looked, live at a link on their phone, starts a different conversation: not "would you like a website?" but "this already exists, want it?" Each demo you make also becomes portfolio for the next call.

The call and the follow-up

The demo earns you the right to a short, honest call: you saw their site, you built them something better, it's £X a month done-for-you, no setup fee. Most won't say yes on the first contact. The money is in polite, spaced follow-up over the following weeks, because you're often just waiting to be there in the week their old site finally embarrasses them in front of a customer. The legal bit nobody teaches

UK business-to-business cold outreach is legal done properly: screen numbers against the Telephone Preference Service and Corporate TPS before calling, follow PECR rules for emails and texts, and handle prospect data under UK GDPR. Gov.uk's direct marketing guidance is the plain-English starting point. Sole traders count as individuals under these rules, so screening isn't optional. None of it is difficult; it just has to become routine before the first call, not after the first complaint.

The three rules that keep outreach legal: screen every number against TPS and Corporate TPS before you dial; follow PECR before any marketing email or text; and keep prospect data minimal, accurate and deletable under UK GDPR. Ten minutes of routine, and it's the part most "AI agency" advice never mentions.

The boring setup and the realistic timeline

In the UK you can start as a sole trader with a single registration on gov.uk, and a realistic timeline from blank laptop to first paying client is four to eight weeks of consistent outreach.

The administrative side is smaller than most people fear, and the timeline is longer than most courses promise. Both facts matter.

Registering and getting paid

Setting up as a sole trader is a free HMRC registration, with no company formation needed on day one. For payment, monthly direct debit (GoCardless and similar) beats chasing invoices: the client signs once and the retainer collects itself. A one-page agreement covering what's included, what's extra and how either side leaves is enough to start.

A week-by-week shape

Weeks one to two: set up the tools, build your own agency site (it's the answer to "have you done this before?") and your demo template. Weeks two to four: build the lead sheet for your territory and start daily calls with demos going out ahead of them. Weeks four to eight: follow-up season. This is where the first yes usually lands, and where most people who fail have already quietly stopped calling.

Who shouldn't start an agency

Skip it if you won't do outreach in any form, if you need income this month rather than this quarter, or if you're shopping for passive income. This is a real service business with real customers, and it compounds precisely because most people won't do the unglamorous fortnight of follow-up calls. If that filter doesn't put you off, the barrier that used to be technical simply isn't there any more.

Starting with no experience: FAQ

Can you really start a web design agency with no coding experience?

Yes. In 2026 the build itself is the easiest part to replace. AI coding agents such as Claude Code produce complete, mobile-first websites from plain-English instructions, so the founder's job shifts to everything around the build: choosing a niche, finding prospects, showing them a demo, agreeing a price and looking after the client. Those are learnable business skills, not technical ones. What AI does not replace is the outreach: someone still has to contact prospects consistently.

How much does it cost to start a web design agency in 2026?

Realistically £25–£50 a month. The main cost is an AI coding subscription (Claude's paid plans start at around £15–18/month; check current rates at anthropic.com/pricing), plus roughly £10 a year per domain. GitHub and static hosting such as Cloudflare Pages are free at this scale. There is no stock to buy and no office needed, which is why the model suits starting alongside a job. The real investment is hours of outreach, not money.

How much should a new web design agency charge?

Charge a monthly done-for-you plan rather than a one-off fee: £50/month for a one-page site and £100–150/month for a full site, with hosting, security and small edits included. Monthly pricing is easier for a small business to say yes to than a £1,500 invoice, it matches what they already pay lead platforms, and ten clients at £100/month is £12,000 a year of recurring revenue that compounds instead of resetting to zero after every build.

How long does it take to get the first paying client?

With consistent outreach, four to eight weeks from a blank laptop is a realistic range: one to two weeks learning the tools and building your own site and demo template, then daily prospecting and calls. Some people close in the first fortnight; others take longer. The honest variable is call volume: the method fails quietly for anyone who builds demos but never contacts the businesses they were built for.

Is cold calling businesses legal in the UK?

Business-to-business cold calling is legal in the UK when done properly. You must screen numbers against the Telephone Preference Service and Corporate TPS registers before calling, and follow PECR rules for electronic messages and UK GDPR for any personal data you hold on prospects. Gov.uk's direct marketing guidance sets out the rules. Sole traders count as individuals under these rules, so screening matters. It is entirely workable; it just has to be done by the book.
