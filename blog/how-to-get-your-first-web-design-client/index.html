How to get your first web design client, with no ads and no portfolio

Published July 2026 10 min read

The advice you keep reading is to build a portfolio, set up ads and wait. That order is exactly backwards, and it is why most people never land a first client. Nobody local is searching for a brand-new web designer, and a portfolio of sites nobody asked for is a folder you show to people who are not there. The businesses you actually want are sitting in plain view: a plumber, a barber, a garage with a site that looks broken on a phone, or no site at all. The fastest route to a first paying client in 2026 is not to advertise, it is to pick one of those businesses, spend an afternoon letting an AI build them a better site, and put that demo in front of them. You are not asking them to imagine your work, you are showing them their own name on something that looks ten years newer. Thirty of those approaches, done properly, is a first client. This is how to do it without spending a penny on ads.

Quick answer

To get your first web design client with no ads or portfolio, build a lead sheet of 20 to 40 local businesses that have a mobile number and a dated or missing website, then send the most promising handful a live demo built for them before you pitch anything. Reach out by phone or a personal message with a five-line opener, let the demo do the selling, and follow up twice. Thirty focused approaches typically produce a first paying client inside a month, at a cost of your time rather than an ad budget.

Why ads and portfolios are the wrong first move

A first web design client comes from targeted direct outreach to businesses with a visibly dated site, not from paid ads or a speculative portfolio, because you can solve a specific problem you have already spotted before you ever make contact.

The instinct to look "ready" before you start is what kills momentum. You do not need to look established. You need one business to see a version of their site that is obviously better than what they have.

Ads send you strangers, outreach sends you problems you can solve

Paid ads cost money you do not have yet and deliver people who clicked, not people you chose. Targeted outreach is the opposite: you pick the business because you can already see the problem, which means every conversation starts from a place of "I noticed something" rather than "please hire me". That framing does more for your close rate than any budget.

The demo is the portfolio

A portfolio proves you built something for someone else. A demo in the prospect's own name proves you can build it for them, which is the only question a first-time buyer actually has. Because an AI coding tool can produce a real, clickable site in a couple of hours, you can afford to make the portfolio piece be the pitch. The mechanics of that build are covered in can AI actually build client websites.

Build the lead sheet: who to target and how to find them

The best first clients are local service businesses with a mobile number and a dated, broken-on-mobile or entirely missing website, because they have money coming in, a clear problem, and no in-house marketing team to get past.

A lead sheet is just a short list of businesses that fit a tight bar, with the details you need to reach them. Quality of list beats size every time. Thirty right-fit businesses will out-convert three hundred random ones.

Where to find them

Google Maps for your town plus a trade ("electrician", "hairdresser", "garage") surfaces dozens of candidates with their site and number attached. Local directories, Facebook business pages and the "sponsored by" lists on club and school websites are all rich with owner-run businesses. Open each site on your phone: if it is slow, ugly or missing, it goes on the sheet.

Record only what you will use

For each lead: business name, what they do, the phone number, the current site (or "none"), and one line on what is wrong with it. That last column is your opener written in advance. Keep the sheet to 20 to 40 names so you can actually work it, not admire it.

Build the demo before you pitch

Pick the three or four most promising leads and build each a real, live demo of their new site first, because a working page in their own name converts far better than a message asking them to picture what you could do.

This is the step everyone skips and the reason it works. You are inverting the normal sales order: you deliver a slice of the product before asking for the meeting.

Keep the demo real and specific

Use their actual business name, real photos from their existing pages or reviews, their services and their town. A generic template with their name pasted on top does not land the way a page that clearly knows their business does. It only needs to be one strong page, not a finished site, because its job is to earn a conversation.

Host it somewhere you can send a link

Put each demo on a free static host so you have a clean URL to share. The whole point is that the prospect taps a link on their phone and sees themselves, modernised, in ten seconds. How you price the real thing once they are interested is set out in how much to charge for a website.

Reach out: the call, the message, the follow-up

Lead with the demo, not with yourself: a five-line opener that says who you are, what you noticed, and that you have already built a modern version, ending in an offer to send the link, outperforms any polished pitch.

The goal of first contact is small and specific: get a yes to seeing the demo. That is it. The demo then does the heavy lifting, so you do not need to be a natural salesperson.

The legal bit, briefly: screen your call list against the Telephone Preference Service and its corporate register (the CTPS) and stop calling anyone opted out. Marketing email and text to sole traders is treated as consumer contact under PECR, so it generally needs consent or a prior relationship. Follow the ICO's direct marketing guidance rather than guessing, and keep B2B phone calls to numbers not on the TPS as your safe default.

Follow up twice, then move on

Most first clients come from the second or third contact, not the first. If they went quiet after seeing the demo, a short "did you get a chance to look" a few days later recovers a real share of them. Two follow-ups is the sweet spot: persistent enough to catch the busy ones, not so much you become the nuisance you were trying to save them from.

Use a simple written agreement

When someone says yes, put the basics in writing: what you will deliver, the monthly fee, what is included, and how either side ends it. It protects you both and it signals you run a real business. Turn one client into a pipeline (and who this is not for)

The first client is a system, not luck: once thirty approaches with a real demo produce one yes, you repeat the same lead sheet, demo and follow-up loop, and each finished client becomes a genuine reference for the next.

The value of the first client is not the fee, it is that it proves the loop works and gives you a real site to point at. From there you are refining a machine, not starting from zero each time.

Every client is proof for the next three

Once you have one live site earning a monthly fee, your outreach changes from "I could build you one" to "I built this for a business near you, here it is". A single reference in the same town does more than any amount of self-description, and it compounds as you add clients.

Ask for the local referral

Owner-run businesses know other owner-run businesses. A happy client who is paying you a small monthly fee is usually glad to name the friend whose site is also a mess. One good referral can be worth thirty cold approaches, so ask, plainly, once the first site is live and they are pleased.

Who this is not for

If you will not pick up the phone or send the message, none of this works, and no amount of demo quality rescues it. This is an outreach business with a build attached, not a build business that markets itself. It also is not for anyone hoping to stay anonymous behind a website and wait for inbound: the whole method depends on you being the one who reaches out first. If that part is a hard no, a different model suits you better.

Getting your first web design client: FAQ

How do I get my first web design client with no portfolio?

You build the portfolio piece for them before you ask for a penny. Pick one local business with a dated site, spend a couple of hours letting an AI coding tool build a better version, and send them that live demo. A working page in their own name beats a folder of other people's work, because it removes the only real objection a first-time client has, which is proof that you can actually do it. Ten of these demos aimed at ten well-chosen businesses will get you a first client faster than months of building a general portfolio nobody asked to see.

How do I get web design clients without paid ads?

Direct outreach to a short, specific list, not a spray of adverts. Build a lead sheet of roughly 20 to 40 local businesses that have a mobile number and a dated or missing website, then reach each one with a personalised message and a demo built for them. Ads cost money you do not have yet and send you strangers who did not ask; a targeted demo costs an afternoon and lands in front of a business with a problem you have already half-solved. One good client from thirty focused approaches is a normal, repeatable result.

Is cold calling businesses for web design legal in the UK?

Cold calling a business landline for a genuine business-to-business offer is generally lawful, but you must screen the list against the Telephone Preference Service and its corporate register, the CTPS, and stop calling anyone who has opted out. Marketing email and text to sole traders and small partnerships is treated as consumer contact under PECR, so it usually needs prior consent or a prior-relationship exemption. The safe route for a beginner is B2B phone calls to numbers not on the TPS, following the ICO's guidance rather than guessing.

What should I say to a business to get web design work?

Keep it short and lead with the demo, not with yourself. Say who you are in one line, that you noticed their site looks dated on a phone, that you have already built a modern version they can look at, and offer to send the link. Do not pitch price on the first contact and do not read a script robotically. The point of the call is to earn a look at the demo, because the demo does the selling. A five-line opener that ends in a yes to seeing the link is worth more than a polished monologue.

How long does it take to get a first web design client?

For someone who actually does the outreach, days to a few weeks, not months. The work is not talent, it is volume and follow-up: a lead sheet of thirty businesses, a demo built for the most promising handful, a first round of calls, and a follow-up on the ones who went quiet. Most beginners who fail do not fail at building, they fail at sending. If you make thirty genuine approaches with a real demo attached and follow up twice, a first paying client is a realistic outcome inside the first month.
