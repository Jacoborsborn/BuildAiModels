The best niche for a web design agency (it's not SaaS)

Published July 2026 10 min read

Ask the internet for the best niche for a web design agency and you will get the same three answers, all wrong for a beginner: SaaS startups, e-commerce brands, restaurants. They sound modern and lucrative, which is exactly why every other new agency chases them and drowns. The niche that actually works for someone starting from zero is the one nobody puts on a mood board: local trade businesses, the plumbers and electricians and roofers whose websites still look like 2013 on a phone. There are more than five million small businesses in the UK, and a huge slice of them are exactly this: reachable, unglamorous, and already paying £60–120 a month to lead platforms for shared work they do not own. A niche is not a preference; it is a filter that makes your outreach possible. Pick the wrong one and you spend three months talking to people who cannot be reached and would never pay monthly anyway. Here is how to pick the right one, and why the boring answer wins.

Quick answer

The best niche for a web design agency in 2026 is local trade businesses, plumbers, electricians, roofers and decorators, not startups or SaaS. They have constant local demand, websites often built a decade ago, and a habit of paying monthly for marketing. Qualify on two signals, a dated site and a mobile number, and one UK county holds hundreds of prospects at £50–150 a month.

The best niche is local trades, not the trendy ones

For a new agency in 2026, the best niche is local trade businesses inside one territory, because their demand is constant, their websites are visibly dated, and they already pay monthly for marketing, so a recurring plan is a familiar idea rather than a new one.

This is the decision that quietly decides whether your outreach works at all, and it is worth making before you build a single demo.

What a niche actually is

A niche is not the type of website you make; it is the type of customer you call. "Anyone with a business" feels like more opportunity, but it makes every part of the job harder: a different pitch every call, a different demo every time, no reusable knowledge. A real niche is narrow enough that the second call is easier than the first because you have heard the objections before and your template already fits. Constraint is not a limit on the opportunity. It is what makes the work repeatable.

Why local trades specifically

Trades tick every box a beginner needs. Their work is local and always in demand, so they are not going out of business next quarter. An enormous number of them have a site built years ago on an old builder, or nothing beyond a Facebook page. And they are used to paying monthly, often grudgingly, to platforms like Checkatrade for shared leads. You are not selling them a new concept. You are offering a better deal, an asset with their own name on it, on a bill they already accept.

The three tests a niche must pass

Before you commit to any niche, run it through three questions. Can you reach the decision-maker directly? Do they already pay monthly for something comparable? Is there a visible problem you can show them in ten seconds? Local trades pass all three. Most trendy niches fail at least one, which is why they feel exciting on paper and hollow on the phone.

Why local trades beat startups, SaaS and restaurants

Startups and SaaS founders are web-literate, price sensitive and often build in-house; restaurants run thin margins and are chased by every agency in town; local trades are reachable, monthly-billing and under-served, which is why they convert for beginners and the trendy niches do not.

It helps to see the popular niches lined up against the tests that actually matter, rather than against how good they sound in a pitch deck.

The trendy niches, honestly

SaaS and startups know exactly what a website costs and usually have someone in-house who can open a template, so a done-for-you plan is a tough sell to the one audience least likely to need it. Restaurants have famously thin margins, a high closure rate, and an inbox full of agencies already. E-commerce brands want conversion-rate optimisation and integrations, not a simple brochure site, so the job balloons past what a beginner should take on. None of these are impossible. They are just the wrong first fight.

Boring is the whole point

The reason this niche stays open is that it is unglamorous. Nobody starts an agency dreaming of building a roofer's website, so the space is not crowded with talented competitors the way the trendy niches are. The same instinct that makes most new agencies skip trades is exactly what leaves the opportunity sitting there for the person willing to make the calls.

The qualification bar: a dated site and a mobile number

Within local trades, only two signals matter: a visibly dated website (the problem you can show them) and a mobile number on that site (the way you actually reach the owner). A prospect must clear both before they earn a place on your calling sheet.

A niche is the pond; the qualification bar is how you avoid casting into the empty half of it. This one rule removes most of the wasted effort beginners burn through.

Why the dated site matters

A site built a decade ago, or one that pinches and scrolls sideways on a phone, is a problem the owner can see the moment you point at it. You are not arguing that they need a website; they have one, and it is embarrassing them. That turns a cold pitch into a demonstration. The rebuild itself now takes an evening, so you can show them the fixed version before you have even spoken.

Why the mobile number matters

A mobile number on the site means you can reach the person who decides, with no receptionist and no info@ inbox where messages quietly die. This single detail separates a prospect you can close from a logo you can only admire. The numbers: how big the market is and what a patch is worth

The UK has more than five million small businesses, a large share of them local trades, so a single county holds hundreds of qualified prospects; you only need ten at £100 a month to reach £12,000 a year of recurring revenue.

The fear that a narrow niche is too small gets the maths backwards. The niche is never the bottleneck. Your call volume is.

How many prospects a county holds

The UK's business population runs to well over five million, the overwhelming majority of them micro-businesses, per figures the Federation of Small Businesses tracks. Filter one county to five or six trades, then again for a dated site and a mobile number, and you are still left with hundreds of qualified names. A solo operator cannot service more than a few dozen retainers well, so you will never run out of prospects before you run out of hours.

What a single patch is worth

The retainer maths compounds quietly. Ten clients at £100 a month is £12,000 a year that renews instead of resetting; twenty is £24,000, and that is from a fraction of one county in one trade. Why the recurring model needs this niche

Trades stay because leaving means their site goes dark and their edits stop. That low churn is what makes the recurring model hold up, and it is the same reason done-for-you retainers are the profitable end of web design now, while the one-off build has collapsed in price.

Narrow further: one trade, one patch, then widen

Do not stop at "local trades"; start with one trade in one area, because a single-trade focus lets you reuse the same demo, the same photos and the same three objections on every call, and each conversation gets sharper instead of starting from scratch.

The instinct to keep the net wide feels safer, but a narrower start is what makes the first month survivable.

The single-trade advantage

A demo built for electricians, with the right photos, the right services and the right words, beats a generic small-business site on every call, because it looks like you already understand their world. Specialising also compounds your credibility: "I build sites for electricians" is a reason to listen, where "I build websites" is background noise. You reuse one template and get better at one pitch, fast.

Sizing the territory

A county, or a city and its surrounding towns, is the right size: big enough to hold hundreds of qualified prospects, small enough that "I'm based near you" is true on every call. Local familiarity closes deals. Naming their town, knowing their patch and being someone they could theoretically meet all lower the guard on a cold call.

When and how to widen

Once you have a handful of clients in one trade, the same territory usually holds four or five adjacent trades, roofers next to the electricians, decorators next to the plumbers, that you can move into with almost no new learning. You widen the trade, not the geography, then widen the geography once the trades are covered. Each expansion reuses everything you already built.

Who this niche is not for

Skip local trades if you want a portfolio that impresses other designers, if you find phone calls unbearable, or if you are only interested in clients who will pay a headline figure once. This is a volume-and-relationship game with unfashionable customers, and it works precisely because most people who could do it would rather chase the glamorous niche and lose.

Choosing a niche: FAQ

What is the best niche for a new web design agency in 2026?

Local trade businesses: plumbers, electricians, roofers, decorators, joiners and the like, inside one defined territory. Their demand is local and constant, an enormous number of them run websites built a decade ago or none at all, and they already pay monthly for marketing on lead platforms, so a recurring plan is a familiar shape rather than a new idea. You are not educating them about why being findable matters. You are offering a better deal on something they already buy, which is a far shorter conversation than any trendy niche allows.

Why not target startups, SaaS companies or restaurants?

Because they fail the tests that make a niche workable for a beginner. Startups and SaaS founders are web-literate, price sensitive and often build in-house, so a done-for-you monthly plan is a hard sell. Restaurants have thin margins, high closure rates and are chased by every agency in town. Trendy niches look attractive but they are crowded, hard to reach and slow to pay. Local trades are unglamorous, reachable by phone and used to monthly bills, which is exactly why they convert.

How do I know if a niche is worth targeting?

Run it through three tests. First, can you reach the decision-maker directly, ideally by a mobile number on their own website, with no gatekeeper. Second, do they already pay monthly for something comparable, so recurring pricing feels normal. Third, is there a visible, undeniable problem you can show them, such as a site that looks a decade old on a phone. A niche that passes all three converts. A niche that fails any one of them will drain your first three months for very little return.

Should I pick one trade or serve all local businesses?

Start with one trade in one area, then widen once it works. A single-trade focus lets you reuse the same demo template, the same photos style and the same three objections on every call, so each conversation gets sharper instead of starting from scratch. It also makes your pitch specific: a site built for electricians beats a generic small-business site every time. Once you have a handful of clients in that trade, the same territory usually holds four or five adjacent trades you can move into with almost no new learning.

How many clients can one local niche realistically hold?

More than a solo operator can service. One UK county typically holds hundreds of qualified trade businesses across five or six trades once you filter for a dated site and a reachable number. You do not need to win most of them. Ten clients at £100 a month is £12,000 a year of recurring revenue, and twenty is £24,000, from a fraction of a single county. The constraint is never the size of the market. It is how many calls you are willing to make and follow up.
